EMI Capital Requirements Explained

An EMI needs EUR 350,000 of initial capital at authorisation (EMD2 Art 4). It then holds own funds of at least the higher of that figure and 2% of its average outstanding e-money, and its regulator may raise or lower the calculated amount by up to 20% (EMD2 Art 5). National laws mostly repeat these euro figures, but Hungary states HUF 100 million and Liechtenstein allows Swiss francs.

Written by Jan Hagen, regulatory compliance expert. Jan has supported authorisations with the FCA and DNB and has held senior compliance and MLRO roles.

Last checked against official sources: 23 November 2026

Checked against the EMD2 and PSD2 texts and national sources on 23 November 2026.

What is the initial capital for an EMI?

EMD2 Art 4 requires "not less than EUR 350,000" at the time of authorisation, made up of the own funds items listed in Art 57(a) and (b) of Directive 2006/48/EC. The regulator checks evidence of the capital with the file: PSD2 Art 5(1)(c), applied to EMIs by EMD2 Art 3(1), asks for evidence that the institution holds the initial capital.

Several regulators state how the money must be held:

  • Hungary: HUF 100 million in cash, held in an unrelated credit institution until operations start (MNB guide, Act CCXXXV s 11(4), (6)).
  • Austria: EUR 350,000 freely available to the managers in Austria (E-Geldgesetz section 4(3)).
  • Poland: the money must be free of credit, loans and other encumbrances (Art 132b(2)).
  • Spain: initial capital is not the same as share capital (Banco de España guide, 2.5).

How are own funds calculated?

Initial capital is checked at authorisation. Own funds apply from then on, and EMD2 Art 5(1) says they must not fall below the higher of the initial capital and the amount calculated under Art 5.

Method D covers e-money issuance. Own funds must be at least 2% of average outstanding e-money (Art 5(3)). Average outstanding e-money is measured over the preceding six months (EMD2 Art 2(4)). Where the figure is not known in advance, the regulator may use a representative portion based on historical data, or the projections in your business plan if the history is too short (Art 5(4)).

For payment services that are not linked to e-money, the regulator sets Method A, B or C, and your own funds must be at least the sum of both requirements (Art 5(2)).

  • Method A: 10% of fixed overheads of the preceding year, adjustable after a material change in the business (PSD2 Art 9(1)).
  • Method B: slices of one twelfth of the previous year's payment volume, from 4.0% of the slice up to EUR 5 million down to 0.25% above EUR 250 million, times the scaling factor k (Art 9(1)).
  • Method C: slices of a relevant income indicator, from 10% down to 1.5%, times k (Art 9(1)). The factor k is 0.5 for money remittance only and 1 for services 1 to 5 (Art 9(2)).

The regulator picks the method under national law (PSD2 Art 9(1)).

What is the 20% adjustment?

After reviewing your risk management, loss data and internal controls, the regulator may require own funds up to 20% higher than the calculated amount, or allow up to 20% lower (EMD2 Art 5(5)). The adjustment applies to the Art 5 calculation. The EUR 350,000 floor in Art 5(1) still applies.

What do the numbers look like?

The arithmetic below uses only the EMD2 rules and invented volumes, so treat it as an illustration.

Average outstanding e-money 2% (Method D) Own funds floor If the regulator adds 20% If it takes off 20%
EUR 10 million EUR 200,000 EUR 350,000 EUR 350,000 EUR 350,000
EUR 17.5 million EUR 350,000 EUR 350,000 EUR 420,000 EUR 350,000
EUR 30 million EUR 600,000 EUR 600,000 EUR 720,000 EUR 480,000

Up to EUR 17.5 million of average outstanding e-money the EUR 350,000 floor controls, because 2% of that figure equals EUR 350,000. Above it, the 2% calculation controls. If the same firm also provides unrelated payment services under Method A with fixed overheads of EUR 1,000,000, the Method A amount is EUR 100,000, and own funds of at least the sum of the two requirements follow Art 5(2), still subject to the EUR 350,000 floor.

How do national rules differ?

The euro figure is the same in every state that sets one. The differences are in currency, form and extra conditions.

Country What differs Source
Hungary EMI start-up capital stated as HUF 100 million in cash. PI capital HUF 37.5 million, HUF 15 million for payment initiation only, HUF 6 million for cash transfers only MNB EMI guide; MNB PI guide
Liechtenstein EMI: EUR 350,000 or the Swiss franc equivalent. PI: CHF 20,000, 50,000 or 125,000, or the euro equivalent FMA WL 2018/18; FMA WL 2019/8
Poland EUR 350,000 in PLN at the NBP average rate on the day the licence is issued Act Art 132b(1)
Romania EUR 350,000 in lei equivalent Law 210/2019 Art 17
Iceland ISK equivalent of EUR 350,000 Act 17/2013 Art 11
Latvia Own funds of at least EUR 350,000 plus the Section 35 requirement Latvijas Banka
Spain Own funds for e-money: the higher of EUR 350,000 and 2% of e-money in circulation over six months Banco de España guide, 2.3
Croatia Small EMI: EUR 119,000 capital, Croatia only. Full EMI: EUR 350,000 HNB consolidated Act
Lithuania Restricted EMI: no minimum capital, valid only in Lithuania. Full EMI: EUR 350,000 lb.lt
United Kingdom Authorised EMI EUR 350,000. Small EMI: 2% of average outstanding e-money once it reaches EUR 500,000 EMRs Sch 2 paras 2, 3(1)

The Hungary EMI figure is what the MNB guide states in section 3.2. EMD2 sets the Union floor at EUR 350,000, and the same MNB guide refers to EUR 350,000 when it lists the proof of start-up capital (section 4.5.5). We have not converted the forint figure and have found no MNB statement on how the two relate, so ask the MNB which amount binds before you plan. The Lithuania restricted EMI terms are as recorded on our Lithuania page, because the Bank of Lithuania website could not be opened for the original check.

Country pages carry the rest: Hungary, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Croatia, Poland and Latvia.

Can a small EMI avoid the EUR 350,000?

Sometimes. EMD2 Art 9(1) lets a Member State waive Arts 3, 4, 5 and 7, in whole or in part, for legal persons whose average outstanding e-money stays within a national limit of at most EUR 5,000,000 and whose managers have no convictions for money laundering, terrorist financing or other financial crimes. Each state decides whether to offer it. A small EMI cannot passport. The UK has its own small EMI rules under EMRs Sch 2. The EMI vs payment institution guide compares the tiers.

Will PSD3 change EMI capital?

Not yet. The Commission proposed EUR 400,000 for electronic money services (COM(2023) 366, Art 5(d)). The Council's first-reading position of 29 October 2026 sets EUR 250,000 for the issuance of electronic money, now a payment service under a single payment institution licence (Art 5(d)), and adds the minimum amounts together when a firm provides more than one tier. Parliament has not yet voted at second reading, so EMD2 and EUR 350,000 apply today. See the PSD3 and PSR guide.

What this means for you

  • Pay in EUR 350,000, or the national equivalent, before you file, and keep the evidence of where it came from.
  • Model your own funds with 2% of the volumes you expect, and compare that to EUR 350,000. Below EUR 17.5 million of average outstanding e-money, the floor is what binds.
  • Add the Method A, B or C amount if you provide unrelated payment services, and ask your regulator which method it applies.
  • Leave room for the regulator's 20% adjustment in your funding plan.
  • Check the local rules on currency, form and where the money sits before you transfer it.
  • Compare states with the best country guide once capital is settled.

EMI capital FAQs

How much capital does an EMI need?

At least EUR 350,000 at authorisation (EMD2 Art 4), and afterwards own funds at the higher of that and 2% of average outstanding e-money (Art 5).

What are Methods A, B, C and D?

Methods A, B and C are the PSD2 Art 9 calculations for payment services: fixed overheads, payment volume slices, or an income indicator. Method D is the EMD2 Art 5(3) rule for e-money, at least 2% of average outstanding e-money. The regulator sets A, B or C, and you add D.

Can the regulator change my own funds requirement?

Yes, by up to 20% higher or 20% lower than the calculated amount, based on its review of your risk management, loss data and internal controls (EMD2 Art 5(5)).

Is EUR 350,000 the same in every country?

It is the EU minimum, and most states state it in euro. Hungary states HUF 100 million in its MNB guide, Liechtenstein accepts the Swiss franc equivalent, and Poland, Romania and Iceland convert from euro.

What capital does a payment institution need instead?

EUR 125,000, EUR 50,000 or EUR 20,000, depending on the service (PSD2 Art 7). Liechtenstein states these as CHF 125,000, 50,000 and 20,000. Hungary states HUF 37.5 million, 15 million and 6 million.

Does the capital have to stay in a bank account?

EMD2 Arts 4 and 5 set amounts and do not say where the money sits. Hungary requires cash held in an unrelated credit institution until operations start, and Austria requires the capital to be freely available to the managers in Austria. Ask your regulator what it accepts.
