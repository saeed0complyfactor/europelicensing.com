Getting a Safeguarding Bank Account as a New EMI

EU law does not name a bank or promise you an account. It requires that customer funds sit in a separate account at a credit institution (or, at a central bank's discretion, a central bank) or are covered by insurance, and the EBA authorisation guidelines list a draft contract with the credit institution among the safeguarding documents you file. The EBA has published evidence that payment and e-money firms have had accounts refused or closed, and what it recommended.

What does the law require of the safeguarding account?

PSD2 Article 10(1), as replaced by Article 3(1) of Regulation (EU) 2024/886, applies to payment institutions and EMIs. For the segregation method it requires four things of the account.

  1. The funds are never commingled with the funds of anyone other than the users they are held for.
  2. If you still hold them at the end of the business day after you received them, they go into a separate account in a credit institution, into a central bank at the discretion of that central bank, or into secure, liquid, low-risk assets that your home regulator has defined.
  3. They are insulated, under national law, against claims of your other creditors, in particular in your insolvency.
  4. For funds paid in by card or another payment instrument for e-money, safeguarding starts when the funds are credited to you, and at the latest five business days after the e-money is issued (EMD2 Article 7(1)).

The text does not say that the account must be at a bank in your home state, that it must have a particular name, or that you may hold only one. National law and your regulator's guidance fill those gaps, and EMD2 Article 7(4) lets Member States or their regulators decide the method.

The insurance route avoids the account question for customer funds. It needs a policy or guarantee from an insurer or credit institution outside your group, for an amount equal to what you would have segregated. You will still need operating accounts and the account that holds your initial capital.

What do regulators ask to see before licensing?

The EBA Guidelines on authorisation (EBA/GL/2017/09), Guideline 7, list what the safeguarding description contains when you segregate funds:

  • the number of persons with access to the safeguarding account, and their functions;
  • how you administer and reconcile the account so that users' funds stay insulated from your other creditors;
  • a copy of the draft contract with the credit institution;
  • an explicit declaration that you comply with PSD2 Article 10;
  • where relevant, your investment policy for liquid, secure, low-risk assets.

A draft contract means you need a bank in conversation with you before you file. Some regulators go further and ask for proof of an account or a commitment from the bank, and many ask where your initial capital is held.

What has the EBA said about banks and payment or e-money firms?

The EBA published its Opinion on de-risking (EBA/Op/2022/01) on 5 January 2022, with an annexed report (EBA/REP/2022/01). It defines de-risking as decisions by institutions to refuse or end relationships with customers in certain risk categories, and says it can be legitimate risk management or a sign of ineffective ML/TF risk management.

On payment and e-money firms, the report records the following. Most of it is what firms and regulators told the EBA, not the EBA's own finding about any bank.

  • E-money institutions told the EBA that the main argument banks used to end a relationship was the bank's risk appetite and a perception that EMIs could not meet their AML/CFT obligations (report para 23).
  • Trade associations said members had been de-risked after eight to ten years, sometimes at one or two months' notice, and that appeal routes were opaque (paras 25 and 26).
  • Some firms kept several bank accounts when they could, to keep their business running (para 27).
  • Competent authorities in states with many payment and e-money firms shared evidence of multiple closures that they considered unwarranted.
  • The EBA found that the Article 36 duty to notify the regulator of a rejection was applied inconsistently, and that notifications to regulators did not match what firms said had happened.

The report says the scale of de-risking cannot be quantified because no complete statistics exist, and that many cases may go unreported. Its evidence comes from information-gathering exercises in 2020 and 2021 and does not describe what any bank does today.

The EBA recommended that the Commission clarify PSD2 Article 36 in the PSD2 review. It suggested the Commission consider technical standards with a notification template, and extending the notification duty from rejections at onboarding to decisions to end an existing relationship. It also noted that institutions deciding to de-risk may face conflicting provisions of EU law, including PSD2. In 2023 it issued Guidelines EBA/GL/2023/04 on managing ML/TF risks when providing access to financial services. They are addressed to credit and financial institutions and cover all types of customers, and the EBA said it would handle payment institutions in the PSD2 review.

What can you do if a bank refuses to open the account?

PSD2 Article 36 says Member States must make sure payment institutions have access to credit institutions' payment account services on an objective, non-discriminatory and proportionate basis, and that the access is sufficiently extensive to let them provide payment services in an unhindered and efficient manner. A credit institution that rejects an application must give the competent authority duly motivated reasons.

Three limits apply.

  • The wording names payment institutions. The EBA report discusses payment and e-money institutions together, so ask your regulator and local counsel how your Member State treats an EMI.
  • The EBA noted that the notification duty is limited to the onboarding stage and that the provision is high level, which gave rise to divergent application across the EU.
  • The right is to access on objective, non-discriminatory and proportionate terms. It does not entitle you to an account at a particular bank.

One Member State, the EBA report records, introduced a template for banks to notify the regulator of rejected accounts, and the regulator used the data to issue guidance on the rights of payment and e-money firms to bank accounts (report Box 9). The report does not name it.

If your safeguarding account is refused, ask the bank for its reasons in writing and tell your regulator. For access to payment systems, PSD2 Article 35(3), inserted by Regulation (EU) 2024/886, requires a participant in a designated system that lets a PSP use it to give other authorised PSPs the same opportunity on objective, proportionate and non-discriminatory terms, with full reasons for any rejection.

What about the UK?

The FCA's safeguarding rules, in force since 7 May 2026, require a firm that appoints a third party to hold relevant funds to do due diligence on it, including its expertise and market reputation, and to consider diversification (CASS 15.6). CASS 15.7 also covers acknowledgement letters, which put the bank on notice of customers' interests in the relevant funds it holds. See the UK EMI guide and the safeguarding guide.

What this means for you

  1. Decide the safeguarding method with your regulator before you approach banks. The method determines what you ask the bank for.
  2. Open conversations with banks while you write the application. You need a draft contract for Guideline 7, and some regulators want a commitment letter.
  3. Prepare the file the bank needs: your business plan, flows of funds, AML policies and the people who will have access. The bank has its own AML duties to meet.
  4. Approach more than one bank. The EBA report records firms holding several accounts to protect continuity.
  5. Check capital deposit rules in your target country. Several regulators want the initial capital held at an unrelated credit institution until the licence is granted.
  6. Keep every refusal in writing. Article 36 gives your regulator a role in rejections, at least for payment institutions.

EMI bank account FAQs

Does an EMI need a safeguarding account?

If it uses the segregation method, yes: a separate account in a credit institution, or in a central bank at the central bank's discretion, or secure low-risk assets (PSD2 Article 10(1)). The alternative is insurance or a guarantee from outside your group.

Can a bank refuse to open an account for a payment institution?

PSD2 Article 36 requires access on objective, non-discriminatory and proportionate terms, and a bank that rejects must give the regulator duly motivated reasons. It does not guarantee an account at a specific bank.

What is de-risking?

The EBA describes it as decisions by institutions to refuse or end business relationships with customers in certain risk categories (EBA Opinion EBA/Op/2022/01, 5 January 2022).

Do I need a bank account before applying for an EMI licence?

EBA Guideline 7 asks for a copy of the draft contract with the credit institution. The Dutch regulator asks for proof of an opened account or a bank-signed commitment letter. Other regulators ask where your capital is held, so check your target country's checklist.

Can I safeguard funds at a central bank?

PSD2 Article 10(1) allows it only at the discretion of that central bank, so the central bank decides.
