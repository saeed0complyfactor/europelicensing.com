Best Country for an EMI License in Europe (2026)

There is no best country for an EMI licence in Europe, because the EU capital is EUR 350,000 everywhere and the differences sit in fees, language, local substance and which tier you can use. This guide compares eight EU states and the UK on those points, using the regulator's own pages, and ends with a pick by firm profile.

Best country for an EMI license in short

  • Capital is the same in every EU state: EUR 350,000 for a full EMI (EMD2 Art 4). The UK uses the same figure for an authorised EMI (EMRs Sch 2 para 2).
  • The decision clock is 3 months from a complete application in all nine jurisdictions (PSD2 Art 12, applied by EMD2 Art 3(1); EMRs reg 9). Preparation and questions from the regulator sit outside it.
  • Published application fees run from none at the Central Bank of Ireland (it charges an annual levy instead) to EUR 30,000 at the CSSF in Luxembourg (Central Bank of Ireland, CSSF fee regulation). BaFin charges by time spent and the ACPR page states no amount, so neither is zero.
  • Substance rules separate the countries more than fees do. Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Malta write down who must sit locally. Lithuania's written rule could not be retrieved, so ask the Bank of Lithuania.
  • A full EMI passports across the EEA. The UK has no passport since 31 December 2020 (FCA).
  • The small or restricted tiers in Lithuania, the Netherlands, France, Ireland and the UK carry no EU passport.

How we compared the countries

We took each figure from the matching country page on this site, which cites the regulator or the law. The columns are the ones that change your budget and your team: regulator, capital, application fee, statutory decision clock, filing language, substance rules and the size of the register where we could cite one.

We did not rank the countries. A ranking needs weights, and the right weights depend on your product, your customers and your staff. The profile section below gives the weights we would ask you about. Nothing here comes from consultancy price lists or press estimates.

Which country suits which firm?

Each profile names the countries that fit a common starting point and what to check before you commit. Treat them as a starting list, since a different product can reverse any of them.

You want a domestic launch before an EU roll-out

Look at the tiers table. A small or restricted licence costs less in fees and, in Lithuania, carries no minimum capital. It cannot passport, so a later move to a full EMI means a new application. If you already know you want EEA customers, skip the tier.

You want an EU passport from a state that accepts English

Lithuania accepts Lithuanian or English filings. Ireland and Cyprus publish English material, and Malta makes its regulations in English only. None of the three states a filing language rule on the pages we read. Germany and France run proceedings in their own language. If your team has no German or French speakers, those two cost more in translation and in daily dealings with the regulator.

You want the lightest fee bill from the regulator

Ireland charges no application fee but an annual levy, minimum EUR 6,625 in the 2026 guide. Lithuania's state fee is EUR 1,463. Malta and Cyprus sit at EUR 10,000 plus, and Cyprus adds a EUR 5,000 pre-application fee. The Netherlands charges EUR 10,400 plus per-person fees, and Luxembourg EUR 30,000. Germany charges by time spent, so ask BaFin for an estimate. The ACPR page states no fee, which is not the same as none, so ask the ACPR. Annual charges differ too: Malta's annual supervisory fee for e-money alone is EUR 25,000 plus a variable part after the first year, Lithuania's is capped at 0.585% of payment services income, and our pages found no published amount for the Netherlands, Cyprus or Germany. Regulator fees are small beside capital, staff and audit, so do not decide on the fee line alone.

You want to run the business from outside the EU

Every state in the table needs a local legal entity with a head office in that state. Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Malta also set out who must sit there. If you plan a thin local team, read the substance column first. Our EMI licence guide lists the documents the regulators ask for.

You serve Germans, French or Dutch customers

A licence from any EEA state lets you serve them by passport. A local licence changes only where your head office sits and which regulator you answer to. Choose it if your management team lives there anyway.

You want UK customers

An EU licence gives no UK access, and a UK licence gives no EU access. Firms that want both apply twice. See FCA authorisation and the UK EMI page.

You may add crypto services later

An EMI can notify its regulator under MiCA Art 60 for custody and transfer of its own e-money tokens. Other crypto-asset services need a separate CASP authorisation. Lithuania's CASP page covers this, and our MiCA CASP licence guide covers the rest. If you need payment services beyond e-money, check that the same licence covers them.

You are unsure whether you need an EMI at all

If you do not issue e-money, a payment institution licence has lower capital. See the payment institution licence guide.

What this means for you

  1. Fix your passport need first. If you need the EEA, you need a full EMI and the tier table drops out.
  2. Fix your team's languages and location next. They decide between the English-friendly states and the ones with local-language proceedings and local management rules.
  3. Treat regulator fees as a small line. Ask each regulator for its pre-application meeting, where it states its current expectations.
  4. Plan for more than 3 months. The statutory clock starts when the file is complete, and the Dutch regulator says total processing tends to be longer.

Best country for an EMI license: FAQs

Which is the cheapest country for an EMI licence?

We cannot name one. Ireland has no application fee but an annual levy of at least EUR 6,625, and Lithuania's state fee is EUR 1,463. Germany charges by time spent and France states no fee, so they cannot be compared on this line. Capital is EUR 350,000 in every EU state, and audit, staff and safeguarding costs differ by your model.

Which country is easiest for an EMI licence?

We found no official source that ranks difficulty, and the decision clock is the same everywhere. The practical differences are filing language, written substance rules and the tier you can use.

Is Lithuania good for an EMI licence?

It offers a restricted tier, a EUR 1,463 state fee and filing in English. We could not retrieve the Bank of Lithuania's written rule on local staff, so ask it at the pre-application meeting. See the Lithuania EMI page.

Can I get an EMI licence in one country and serve the whole EU?

Yes, for a full EMI. You notify your home regulator, which forwards the notice within 1 month, and the host state assesses it within 1 month (PSD2 Art 28). E-money can be distributed abroad through distributors, not agents (EMD2 Art 3). Restricted and small tiers cannot passport.

Does a UK EMI licence work in the EU?

No. UK-EEA passporting ended on 31 December 2020, so a UK EMI needs its own EU licence to serve the EU.

How long does an EMI licence take?

The law gives the regulator 3 months from a complete application. DNB says total processing tends to be longer, and Malta treats silence as a refusal. See the EMI licence guide.

Should I choose an EMI or a payment institution licence?

Choose an EMI if you issue e-money, such as prepaid balances or wallets that hold value. A payment institution licence covers payment services without issuing e-money and has lower capital.
