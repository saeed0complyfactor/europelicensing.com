Small EMI and Small PI: Which Countries Offer Them

EU law lets each Member State decide whether to offer a lighter small EMI or small payment institution tier, so some states have one and some do not. Of the 31 states we cover, 18 have a small EMI tier and 20 have a small PI tier on the sources we read, and no tier we confirmed carries a passport.

Small EMI and small PI in short

  • The EU ceilings are EUR 5,000,000 of average outstanding e-money for a small EMI (EMD2 Art 9) and a EUR 3 million monthly average of payment transactions for a small PI (PSD2 Art 32(1)). A state may set a lower limit, and several do.
  • Both tiers are optional, so a state is free to offer neither (EMD2 Art 9; PSD2 Art 32).
  • A small tier gives no passport. EMD2 Article 9(3) and PSD2 Article 32(3) switch off the passporting provisions.
  • You must have your head office in the state where you carry on business (EMD2 Art 9(2); PSD2 Art 32(2)).
  • If you outgrow the conditions, you have 30 calendar days to apply for full authorisation (EMD2 Art 9(6); PSD2 Art 32(5)).
  • We found no small EMI tier in 11 states, and no small PI tier in 11 states. Where the table says "not confirmed", we could not read an official source for the tier, so check with the regulator.

Sources: EMD2 and PSD2. Checked on 23 November 2026.

What is a small EMI or small payment institution?

A small EMI or small PI is a licence or registration with lighter requirements than full authorisation. The EU directives set an outer limit and leave the details to national law. That is why the table below has so many different names: limited EMI, restricted-activity EMI, EMI with a waiver, simplified authorisation, exemption, registration.

With a small tier you usually pay less in capital, you may skip some of the governance tests, and in a few states you wait less time for a decision. In return, you serve one country only, you cannot passport, and you must move to full authorisation within 30 calendar days if you pass the limits.

The tier also has a ceiling on what you can do. A small PI under PSD2 Article 32 covers services 1 to 6 of Annex I. That leaves out payment initiation (point 7) and account information (point 8), which has its own registration route under Article 33. A small EMI in some states may store at most EUR 150 on one instrument or account.

What the table shows

The limits vary far more than the EU ceilings suggest. Belgium caps a limited EMI at EUR 1,500,000 and Ireland at EUR 1 million. Latvia caps its registered EMI at EUR 2 million, and Slovenia caps its waiver EMI at EUR 200,000. Croatia sits at EUR 265,000. The EUR 5,000,000 figure is only the upper limit.

Capital for small tiers is often unstated or reduced. Croatia sets EUR 119,000 for a small EMI and EUR 6,500 or EUR 39,500 for a small PI. Slovakia sets EUR 175,000 for its limited-scope EMI and EUR 62,500 for its limited-scope payment provider, or EUR 10,000 for remittance only. Portugal sets EUR 50,000 for its exempt payment institution. Latvia and Lithuania set no minimum. Where our page says "not stated", we did not find a figure on the regulator's page or in the law text we read.

Several large licensing markets have no small tier. We found none in Germany, Austria, Spain (for e-money), Hungary or Romania, and none for PIs in Ireland, Germany, Austria or Romania. In those states the entry route is full authorisation.

What this means for you

If you plan to serve one country and stay under the limit, a small tier can cut your capital and sometimes your timeline. Check the services it covers first. A small PI that excludes payment initiation, or a small EMI with a per-instrument storage cap, may not fit your product.

If you plan to serve more than one country, a small tier cannot do it. You need full authorisation to passport. See our passporting guide.

If you are in a state with no small tier, apply for the full licence. For the capital that full tier needs, see the EMI licence and payment institution licence pages.

If you outgrow a small tier, plan the move to full authorisation early. The directive gives you 30 calendar days to apply once the conditions fail, and the full application takes the usual statutory decision period.

Several figures in the table come from regulator pages that we could only partly confirm. The Lithuanian caps, the Greek and Icelandic PI conditions and the Finnish conditions are flagged on the country pages. Confirm them with the regulator before you build a business plan around them.

Small EMI and small PI FAQs

What is a small EMI?

A small EMI is a lighter e-money licence or registration for firms below a national limit on average outstanding e-money, never more than EUR 5,000,000 under EMD2 Article 9. It gives no passport.

What is a small payment institution?

A small PI is a lighter licence or registration for firms whose monthly average of payment transactions over the preceding 12 months stays within a national limit, never more than EUR 3 million under PSD2 Article 32. It gives no passport.

Which countries have a small EMI?

On the sources we read, Belgium, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Slovakia, Slovenia and the UK. Poland has a related route and Cyprus has an enabling provision with no directive.

Does the UK have a small EMI and small PI?

Yes. A UK small EMI is capped at EUR 5m of average outstanding e-money and a small PI at EUR 3m a month. Neither can offer account information or payment initiation services.

Can a small EMI or small PI passport?

No. EMD2 Article 9(3) and PSD2 Article 32(3) exclude the passport provisions.

What happens if I outgrow the limit?

You must seek full authorisation within 30 calendar days of failing the conditions (EMD2 Art 9(6); PSD2 Art 32(5)). Some national laws repeat this, such as Ireland and Denmark.

Is a small tier cheaper?

Often the capital is lower or absent, and some states charge lower fees. For example, Lithuania lists a state fee of EUR 1,235 for a restricted EMI against EUR 1,463 for a full one. The Lithuanian figures come from a page we could not open on the check date, so confirm them first.
