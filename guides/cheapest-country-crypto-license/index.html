Cheapest Country for a Crypto (MiCA) License

For regulator fees, the Banco de Portugal says its CASP application costs nothing, the Czech Republic charges CZK 20,000, and Slovakia, Latvia and Estonia charge from EUR 1,700 to EUR 3,400. Luxembourg, Malta and Liechtenstein charge annual supervision fees that reach tens of thousands. Capital is the same in every EU state, so a low application fee tells you little about what a licence costs to run.

Cheapest country for a crypto license in short

  • Capital under MiCA is EUR 50,000, EUR 125,000 or EUR 150,000 by service class, or a quarter of fixed overheads if higher, in every member state (ESMA, MiCA Art 67 and Annex IV). Capital does not change your choice of country.
  • The lowest published application fees we found are in the Czech Republic (CZK 20,000), Slovakia (EUR 1,700 to 3,400), Latvia (EUR 2,500) and Estonia (EUR 3,000). Croatia prices per service, from EUR 900 for custody alone. Portugal says its application has no associated costs (Banco de Portugal).
  • Among the highest published fixed fees are those in Luxembourg (EUR 30,000 plus EUR 40,000 a year) and Liechtenstein (CHF 30,000 plus a base levy of CHF 50,000 a year for custody, platform or exchange firms).
  • Poland has no route: no authority is designated for CASPs, so there is no Polish fee and no Polish licence (UKNF statement). Romania has no confirmed route either.
  • The decision clock is the same everywhere: 25 working days to check completeness, 40 working days to decide (MiCA Art 63).
  • Fees quoted here come from official fee schedules and regulator pages, and we used no consultancy price lists.

Crypto license capital: the same in every EU country

MiCA sets minimum capital by the services you offer. The three classes in Annex IV are:

Class Minimum capital Services
1 EUR 50,000 Order reception and transmission, advice, execution, placing, transfer, portfolio management
2 EUR 125,000 Class 1 plus custody and exchange
3 EUR 150,000 Class 2 plus operating a trading platform

You hold the higher of the class minimum and one quarter of the preceding year's fixed overheads, using projected overheads in your first year (MiCA Art 67(1), (2)). The class list comes from the Lithuania CASP page, which cites ESMA's Annex IV.

Your services set the class, and some regulators price by class too. Bulgaria, Austria and Malta charge a different fee per class. Cyprus and Croatia charge per service.

The UK is outside MiCA. The FCA's regime has no single capital figure that we could confirm. See the UK crypto authorisation page.

How to read the fee table

No fee found

Portugal says no costs attach to the application or the authorisation. Greece and Romania show no fee because we found no fee schedule or no procedure. Belgium's FSMA page states none. Treat "none found" as a gap in what the regulator published, and ask the authority before you budget.

Small application fee, little or no annual fee found

Slovakia, Latvia, Estonia and the Czech Republic have the lowest published application figures. Latvia and Slovakia add an annual supervision payment, and Latvia's is up to 0.6% of gross crypto-asset income with a EUR 3,000 minimum. Estonia and the Czech Republic show no annual amount in what we read.

Fixed fee, then a large annual bill

Luxembourg (EUR 40,000), Malta (from EUR 12,000 a year for one service, before the volume fee), Liechtenstein (CHF 8,000 to 50,000 base) and France (EUR 10,000 a year against EUR 10,000 at application) put as much or more weight on the annual line than on the application. Cyprus adds a turnover surcharge on top of EUR 5,000 to 20,000 per service.

Fee by effort or by service

The Netherlands charges EUR 200 an hour up to EUR 100,000. Germany charges by time. Croatia, Cyprus and Slovenia price each service. In Croatia (EUR 900 against EUR 10,200) and Cyprus (EUR 10,000 against EUR 30,000), a custody-only business pays far less than a trading platform. Slovenia charges the same EUR 2,820 for either, on top of its EUR 14,100 base.

Does a cheaper regulator mean fewer applicants?

Fees are one signal. A second is how many firms the regulator has already authorised or received notifications from, which hints at how much practice it has with MiCA files. The ESMA interim MiCA register on 23 November 2026 lists these entries by home state, from the country pages (the register mixes Art 63 authorisations and Art 60 notifications for many states):

A passport from any of these states covers the whole EU, so the number of local clients does not limit you. The register size shows roughly how many firms have come through the process. It does not show refusals or how long anyone waited.

Costs the fee table leaves out

The regulator's fee is one line of many. Our pages give no amounts for the lines below, because they depend on your model:

  • company formation and a registered office;
  • at least one EU-resident director, and staff where the regulator requires them locally;
  • translation, where the file must be in the local language;
  • audit;
  • a client-funds account and custody and security infrastructure;
  • DORA-grade ICT and AML tooling;
  • insurance or own funds for the prudential safeguard;
  • advisers.

Local substance and language differ by country. Bulgaria's FSC FAQ describes a registered office in Bulgaria, an executive officer resident there and part of the staff working there, and requires documents in Bulgarian. Estonia takes the file in Estonian and allows English documents if the application says so. Lithuania accepts Lithuanian or English, and we could not retrieve the Bank's written view on local staff. A low fee can sit beside a larger staffing and translation bill. Our Bulgaria crypto license cost guide works one example through, and the MiCA license cost guide lists the first-year cost lines.

What this means for you

  1. Pick your service class first. It sets your capital and, in several countries, your fee.
  2. Remove countries with no working route. Poland has none, and Romania has no confirmed one. A Polish or Romanian founder applies in another state and passports back.
  3. Add the annual line to the application fee for each country you shortlist. Luxembourg, Malta, Liechtenstein and Cyprus look very different once you do.
  4. Check language and local staffing next. Your choice may rest on those.
  5. Ask the regulator for its current fee, because schedules change. Iceland's page is undated, and Bulgaria's and Hungary's amounts come from 2026 instruments.

Cheapest country for a crypto license: FAQs

Which country has the cheapest crypto license?

On published application fees, Portugal states no costs, and the Czech Republic (CZK 20,000), Slovakia (EUR 1,700 to 3,400), Latvia (EUR 2,500) and Estonia (EUR 3,000) have the lowest figures we found. Croatia charges EUR 900 for custody alone. Capital is the same everywhere, and annual fees and local staffing change the real total.

Which is the best country for a crypto license?

It depends on your services, your team and where your clients are. The decision clock is the same (MiCA Art 63) and the passport covers the EU (Art 65), so the choice rests on fees, language, substance and how many firms the regulator has authorised.

Is MiCA capital the same in every country?

Yes. It is EUR 50,000, EUR 125,000 or EUR 150,000 by class, or a quarter of fixed overheads if higher (MiCA Art 67, Annex IV). Only the fees differ.

Can I get a crypto license in Poland?

No. No Polish authority is designated for CASPs, and the Sejm did not override the presidential veto of the act on 4 September 2026. Polish firms authorise in another EU state. See the Poland crypto page.

Does a licence from a cheap country work across the EU?

Yes. You notify your home regulator, which forwards the notice within 10 working days, and you can start on receipt or on the 15th calendar day at the latest (MiCA Art 65).

Is a crypto license in Europe cheaper than in the UK?

Several EU states charge less in application fees than the FCA's GBP 28,150 top category, but the UK is a separate regime with no EU passport. Compare on the same services.

Where can I see the current number of authorised CASPs?

On the ESMA interim MiCA register, which ESMA updates weekly. See also the MiCA CASP licence guide.
