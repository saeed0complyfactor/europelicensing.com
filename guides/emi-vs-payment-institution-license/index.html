EMI vs Payment Institution License: Which Do You Need?

You need an EMI licence if customers hold a balance with you that they can spend later or redeem. You need a payment institution (PI) licence if you only execute, acquire, remit or initiate payments and never hold stored value. Capital runs from EUR 20,000 for a remittance-only PI to EUR 350,000 for an EMI, and of the two only an EMI has a route into MiCA crypto services under Art 60, limited to its own e-money tokens.

How do you decide between an EMI and a payment institution?

Work down these questions in order and stop at the first one that fits your product.

  1. Does a customer hold a balance with you that they can spend later, send to third parties or redeem? That is e-money, defined as stored monetary value issued on receipt of funds and accepted by someone other than the issuer (EMD2 Art 2(2)). Only an authorised issuer may issue it (EMD2 Art 10). You need an EMI licence.
  2. Do you only move money that arrives and leaves within the payment process, such as card acquiring, transfers, direct debits or remittance? Funds a PI receives for payment services are not e-money and not a deposit (PSD2 Art 18(3)). You need a PI licence.
  3. Do you only initiate payments from accounts held elsewhere? That is payment initiation, a PI service with EUR 50,000 of initial capital and mandatory professional indemnity insurance (PSD2 Art 7(b); Art 5(2)).
  4. Do you only read account data for the customer? That is account information. You register instead of applying for a licence (PSD2 Art 33). See the AISP and PISP guide.
  5. Do you want to custody or transfer your own e-money tokens under MiCA? Only an EMI can notify for that (MiCA Art 60(4)). A PI has no equivalent route today.
  6. Is your volume small and national? Check whether your home state offers a small EMI or small PI tier before you size the licence.

If you answer yes to question 1 and also need payment services, one EMI licence covers both, because an EMI may provide any PSD2 payment service (EMD2 Art 6(1)(a)).

How do capital and own funds compare?

An EMI needs EUR 350,000 at authorisation (EMD2 Art 4). A PI needs the amount for its most demanding service (PSD2 Art 7).

Business Initial capital Source
EMI, any activity EUR 350,000 EMD2 Art 4
PI, services 1 to 5 (accounts, transfers, cards, acquiring) EUR 125,000 PSD2 Art 7(c)
PI, payment initiation EUR 50,000 PSD2 Art 7(b)
PI, money remittance only EUR 20,000 PSD2 Art 7(a)
Registered account information provider None PSD2 Art 33

Own funds then run alongside the initial capital. An EMI holds at least 2% of average outstanding e-money, measured over the preceding six months, and the regulator adds Method A, B or C for any payment services not linked to e-money (EMD2 Art 5(2), (3)). A PI uses Method A, B or C, and the regulator may require up to 20% more or allow up to 20% less (PSD2 Art 9). The full mechanics are in the EMI capital requirements guide.

What does safeguarding mean for each licence?

Both licences safeguard customer money with the same two methods: segregation in a separate account or low-risk assets, or an insurance policy or guarantee from a provider outside your group (PSD2 Art 10(1); EMD2 Art 7(1)). They differ in which funds they protect.

  • An EMI safeguards funds received in exchange for e-money, and funds received by payment instrument, such as a card, must be safeguarded no later than five business days after the e-money is issued (EMD2 Art 7(1)).
  • A PI safeguards funds received for payment transactions in services 1 to 6, and funds still held at the end of the business day after receipt go into the safeguarded arrangement (PSD2 Art 10(1)). Payment initiation and account information providers hold no customer funds. They hold professional indemnity insurance (PSD2 Art 5(2), (3)).

A PI may hold only payment accounts used exclusively for payment transactions (PSD2 Art 18(2)). It may not take deposits (Art 18(5)). An EMI may not take deposits (EMD2 Art 6(2)) and may not pay interest on balances (EMD2 Art 12).

Which licence works with MiCA?

MiCA Art 48(1)(a) allows only a credit institution or an EMI to issue an e-money token to the public. MiCA Art 60 then lets certain regulated entities provide crypto-asset services by notification, 40 working days before the first service (Art 60(7)). An EMI may do so only for custody and transfers of the e-money tokens it issues (Art 60(4)). The list in Art 60 names credit institutions, central securities depositories, investment firms, EMIs, UCITS management companies and AIFMs, and market operators. Payment institutions are not on it.

So a PI that wants exchange, broker or wider custody services needs a full CASP authorisation, covered in the MiCA CASP licence service. An EMI that wants anything beyond its own tokens needs one too.

Do the small tiers change the answer?

They can lower capital, but they usually remove the passport.

The EU ceilings are EUR 5,000,000 of average outstanding e-money for a small EMI (EMD2 Art 9(1)) and EUR 3 million of monthly payment volume for a small PI (PSD2 Art 32(1)(a)). Each state decides whether to offer them. A firm that outgrows a tier has to apply for full authorisation, and neither tier passports. If you plan to sell across borders, size the full licence from the start.

What this means for you

  • Wallet, prepaid card, neobank or any product with a spendable balance: EMI.
  • Gateway, acquirer, remittance or payout provider that settles within the payment cycle: PI, with EUR 125,000 or EUR 20,000 depending on the service.
  • Open banking app: registration for account information, EUR 50,000 plus insurance for payment initiation.
  • Both a balance and wider payment services: one EMI licence.
  • Crypto plans: only an EMI has a MiCA notification route, and only for its own e-money tokens.
  • Cross-border from day one: skip the small tiers, because none passports.

A PI that wants to start holding balances needs an EMI licence first. Map every feature to the PSD2 and EMD2 definitions before you pick a home state, and use the best country guide for the next step.

What would PSD3 change?

Nothing yet. The Council adopted its first-reading position on the PSD3 directive on 29 October 2026 and Parliament has not voted at second reading. Under that Council text, which is not law, e-money issuance becomes a payment service (Annex I point 8) with initial capital of EUR 250,000 (Art 5(d)), EMIs are deemed authorised as payment institutions after a transition, and a new MiCA Art 60(4a) would let a payment institution provide crypto-asset services for e-money tokens where they are equivalent to its authorised payment services. Read the PSD3 and PSR guide before you commit capital on the assumption that the two licences stay separate.

EMI vs payment institution FAQs

What is the main difference between an EMI and a payment institution?

An EMI issues e-money, so customers hold a balance with it, and it can provide payment services too. A PI provides payment services only and cannot issue e-money (EMD2 Art 6(1); PSD2 Art 18(3)).

Which needs more capital, an EMI or a PI?

An EMI, at EUR 350,000 (EMD2 Art 4). A PI needs EUR 125,000, EUR 50,000 or EUR 20,000 depending on the service (PSD2 Art 7).

Can a payment institution hold customer balances?

Only in payment accounts used exclusively for payment transactions (PSD2 Art 18(2)). Funds received for payment services are not e-money and not a deposit (Art 18(3)). If the balance is meant to be stored and spent later, you need an EMI licence.

Can a payment institution provide crypto services under MiCA?

Not through the Art 60 notification route, because Art 60 does not list payment institutions. An EMI may notify for custody and transfer of its own e-money tokens only (Art 60(4)). Anything else needs a CASP authorisation.

Can an EMI provide payment services without a second licence?

Yes. An EMI may provide any PSD2 Annex I payment service alongside e-money (EMD2 Art 6(1)(a)).

Do both licences passport?

Yes, by notification to the home regulator (PSD2 Art 11(9), Art 28, applied to EMIs by EMD2 Art 3(1)). Small EMIs and small PIs cannot passport (EMD2 Art 9(3); PSD2 Art 32(3)).
