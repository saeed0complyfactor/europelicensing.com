Payment Institution License in the Netherlands

A payment institution licence [vergunning betaalinstelling] from De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB) lets you run payment accounts, execute transfers, acquire card payments, send remittances or initiate payments from the Netherlands, then passport those services across the EEA. It is for payment firms that do not issue e-money. DNB expects at least two day-to-day policymakers who work from the Netherlands.

Who needs a payment institution license in the Netherlands?

You need a DNB licence to provide payment services in the Netherlands unless you are a bank, an exempt provider or fall under another exclusion. DNB lists the services that need authorisation and the services that do not on its payment services pages. A provider may start only after DNB grants the licence or enters it in the register as exempt.

Another route can fit better:

  • If your product holds a balance for later spending, that is e-money and needs an EMI licence in the Netherlands.
  • If you are small and Dutch-only, the exemption registers you at DNB without a licence. It covers services 1 to 5, a monthly average of payment transactions up to EUR 3 million over the past twelve months, safeguarding of client funds and managers with clean antecedents. Money remittance, payment initiation and account information fall outside it. DNB does not supervise you and you cannot passport.
  • If you only provide account information, PSD2 uses a registration route (Art 33). We could not confirm the Dutch procedure from the DNB pages we read, so ask DNB.
  • If you already hold a licence in another EEA state, you passport into the Netherlands and need no Dutch licence.

Payment institution license requirements in the Netherlands

Capital and own funds

Initial capital is EUR 20,000, EUR 50,000 or EUR 125,000 depending on the services (PSD2 Art 7). Own funds then stay at or above the higher of that figure and your Method A, B or C result (Art 8(1), Art 9). DNB asks for calculations under all three methods and the prudential reporting templates for the three years ahead, plus proof that the minimum capital has been deposited when you apply.

Management and fit and proper

Policymakers must be fit and proper, and you file the assessments with the licence application. Anyone with 10% or more of shares or voting rights, or comparable control, needs a declaration of no-objection [verklaring van geen bezwaar] from DNB. That procedure is separate, has a 62 business day statutory period and is expected to finish before the licence is issued.

Substance

DNB requires that the day-to-day management is in the hands of at least two natural persons who work from the Netherlands, and expects a substantial management board presence there. Describe how a dual-hatted director stays independent from the group. The control structure must be transparent.

AML officer and controls

You apply the Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act [Wwft] and the Sanctions Act 1977. Plan for a named AML compliance officer and a risk assessment of your product. See our AML and compliance programme.

Safeguarding

Choose a customer accounts foundation [stichting derdengelden], a segregated assets account, or insurance or a guarantee (PSD2 Art 10(1), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2024/886). For the first two, DNB asks for proof of an opened bank account or a bank-signed commitment letter that blocks the account until the licence is granted. DNB says this proof is frequently missing, which can lead to significant delays or even dismissal of the application.

Professional indemnity insurance and ICT

Payment initiation and account information providers need professional indemnity insurance or a comparable guarantee (PSD2 Art 5(2), (3)). ICT risk, incidents and outsourcing are assessed under DORA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554.

How to get a payment institution license in the Netherlands

  1. Map each product feature to PSD2 Annex I to fix your capital tier and own-funds method.
  2. Contact DNB's Expert Centre on Market Access before you file, to announce the application and ask about handling time.
  3. Set up a Dutch legal entity, appoint two policymakers who will work from the Netherlands and arrange the safeguarding account.
  4. Pay in the capital and keep the evidence.
  5. Prepare the file from DNB's application form and notes.
  6. Submit digitally through MyDNB.
  7. Phase 1 is the completeness check. DNB generally requests missing documents once. If the file is still incomplete, it may dismiss the application and phase 2 does not start. Fees remain due.
  8. In phase 2, the statutory three-month period runs from the moment the file is complete, and DNB may ask for more information, hold a meeting or screen decision makers. The period is suspended while it waits. If you do not supply requested information, DNB may stop considering the application within four weeks.

DNB says total processing tends to take longer than three months. Preparation and question rounds come on top of the statutory period, and we give you a dated plan after scoping.

After you get the payment institution license

  • DNB enters you in its public register.
  • You report to DNB and keep own funds above the requirement. The AFM supervises conduct.
  • You pay the annual supervision fee.
  • To serve another EEA state, you notify DNB, which forwards the notice within 1 month, and the host state assesses it within 1 month (PSD2 Art 28).
  • A new qualifying holding needs a declaration of no-objection before it completes.

Netherlands payment institution license vs other countries

The Netherlands combines a 3-month statutory clock with a staffing rule that puts two policymakers in the country. To compare, see the payment institution licence in Lithuania, Ireland and Luxembourg. Within the Netherlands, the sibling pages are the EMI licence and the MiCA CASP licence. The EU rules sit in our payment institution licence in Europe guide.

Payment institution license in the Netherlands FAQs

What is a betaalinstelling?

A payment institution licensed by DNB under the Wft to provide payment services. It cannot issue e-money, which needs an electronic money institution licence.

How much capital does a payment institution need in the Netherlands?

EUR 20,000 for money remittance only, EUR 50,000 for payment initiation and EUR 125,000 for services 1 to 5 (PSD2 Art 7). Own funds then follow Method A, B or C.

How much does a payment institution licence cost in the Netherlands?

DNB's application fee is EUR 10,400, with EUR 2,900 and EUR 1,600 per person for the suitability and propriety assessments, under the fee regulation valid from 22 September 2026. Add the capital and running costs. DNB has flagged a change to its fees from 11 January 2027.

How long does it take to get a payment institution licence in the Netherlands?

DNB has three months from a complete application, and says total processing tends to be longer. The clock starts only when the file is complete and stops while DNB waits for information.

Do I need people in the Netherlands?

Yes. DNB expects at least two day-to-day policymakers who work from the Netherlands.

What is the Dutch small payment provider exemption?

A registration for providers of services 1 to 5 with a monthly average of up to EUR 3 million, Netherlands activity only and safeguarded funds. DNB does not supervise exempt providers and they cannot passport.

Can I passport a Dutch payment institution licence?

Yes, by notifying DNB (PSD2 Art 28). An exempt provider cannot.

Do I need an EMI or a payment institution licence?

If customers keep a balance with you for later use, you issue e-money and need an EMI licence. If you only execute payments, a payment institution licence is enough.
