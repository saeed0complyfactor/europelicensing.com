EMI License in the Netherlands

An electronic money institution licence [elektronischgeldinstelling, EGI] from De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB) lets you issue e-money and provide payment services from the Netherlands, then passport them across the EEA. It suits wallets, prepaid and card programmes and neobanks that hold customer balances. Plan the staffing early, because DNB expects at least two day-to-day policymakers who work from the Netherlands.

Who needs an EMI license in the Netherlands?

You need a DNB licence if you issue electronic money on a commercial basis. DNB treats issuing to several customers as an indication of that, and active promotion as enough on its own. A one-off or very incidental issue does not qualify. An EGI can also provide the PSD2 Annex I payment services.

Another route can fit better:

  • If you only move money and never hold a balance for later spending, a payment institution licence in the Netherlands needs less capital.
  • If you are small and Dutch-only, the exemption route registers you at DNB without a licence. You must keep outstanding e-money at EUR 5,000,000 or below, store at most EUR 150 on any instrument or account, use no agents, safeguard funds and have managers with clean antecedents. DNB does not supervise you, and you cannot passport.
  • If you already hold an EMI licence in another EEA state, you passport into the Netherlands by notification and need no Dutch licence.
  • If you also want to custody or transfer your own e-money tokens, an EMI can notify the AFM under MiCA Art 60. Wider services need a Dutch CASP authorisation.

EMI license requirements in the Netherlands

Capital and own funds

A full EGI needs minimum own funds of EUR 350,000 (EMD2 Art 4). DNB then applies 2% of average outstanding e-money (Method D) plus, for payment services unrelated to e-money, the higher result of Methods A, B or C. DNB asks for the three-year prudential reporting templates with your file and requires proof that the statutory minimum capital has been deposited when you apply. It reminds applicants to deduct intangible assets and unaudited current-year profit when calculating own funds.

Management and fit and proper

Policymakers must be fit, shown by education, experience and competence, and proper. You file the assessments together with the licence application. Anyone who holds or acquires 10% or more of your shares or voting rights, or comparable control, needs a declaration of no-objection from DNB [verklaring van geen bezwaar] under Wft section 3:95. That procedure runs separately with a 62 business day statutory period, and DNB expects all qualifying holdings to be approved before it issues your licence.

Substance

DNB requires the day-to-day management to sit with at least two natural persons who work from the Netherlands (the four-eyes principle). It also expects a substantial presence of the management board and key function staff in the Netherlands. Where a director also sits on group entities, you describe how your company stays independent and how conflicts are handled. The control structure must be transparent, with a diagram from the ultimate beneficial owner down.

AML officer and controls

DNB expects you to know the Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act [Wet ter voorkoming van witwassen en financieren van terrorisme, Wwft] and the Sanctions Act 1977. Plan for a named AML compliance officer and a business risk assessment. See our AML and compliance programme.

Safeguarding

You separate customers' funds from your own by one of three methods: a customer accounts foundation [stichting derdengelden], a segregated assets account, or insurance or a guarantee (PSD2 Art 10(1), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2024/886). For the first two, DNB asks for proof of an opened bank account or a commitment letter signed by the bank. The letter confirms that customer acceptance is complete and that the account is blocked until the licence is granted.

ICT and DORA

DNB notes that ICT risk, incident reporting, continuity and outsourcing are now assessed under Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA). Its notes refer to the DNB DORA page for the detail.

How to get an EMI license in the Netherlands

  1. Contact DNB's Expert Centre on Market Access before you file. DNB suggests announcing the application and asking about the expected handling time.
  2. Set up a Dutch legal entity, appoint two policymakers who will work from the Netherlands and open the safeguarding account arrangements.
  3. Pay in the capital and keep the evidence.
  4. Prepare the file from DNB's application form and notes. It includes a business plan, a budget for the first three years, the control structure, the policymaker assessments and the DORA material.
  5. Submit digitally through MyDNB. You receive a confirmation of receipt.
  6. Phase 1 is the completeness check. DNB generally asks for missing documents once. If the file is still incomplete, it may dismiss the application and phase 2 never starts. The fees stay due.
  7. Phase 2 is the substantive assessment. The statutory three-month period runs from the moment the file is complete, and DNB tells you in writing when that is. It is suspended while DNB waits for information. If you do not supply requested information, or supply it insufficiently, DNB may stop considering the application within four weeks.
  8. DNB grants the licence or tells you it intends to reject.

DNB says total processing tends to take longer than three months, since the statutory period only runs once the file is complete. Preparation and question rounds come on top, and we give you a dated plan after scoping.

After you get the EMI license

  • DNB enters you in its public register.
  • You report to DNB under the prudential reporting templates and keep own funds above the requirement. The AFM supervises your conduct.
  • You pay DNB's annual supervision fee.
  • To serve another EEA state, you notify DNB, which forwards the notice. You may use distributors abroad but not agents to issue e-money (EMD2 Art 3(4) and (5)).
  • A new qualifying holding needs a declaration of no-objection before it completes.

Netherlands EMI license vs other countries

The Netherlands asks for two policymakers working from the country, and its small-issuer route is a registration exemption with no passport. To compare, see the EMI licence in Lithuania, Ireland and Luxembourg. Within the Netherlands, the sibling pages are the payment institution licence and the MiCA CASP licence. The EU-wide rules sit in our EMI licence in Europe guide.

EMI license in the Netherlands FAQs

How much capital does an EMI need in the Netherlands?

EUR 350,000 of own funds at authorisation (EMD2 Art 4). DNB then applies 2% of average outstanding e-money, plus a method-based amount for payment services unrelated to e-money.

How much does an EMI licence cost in the Netherlands?

DNB's application fee is EUR 10,400, with EUR 2,900 and EUR 1,600 per person for the suitability and propriety assessments, under the fee regulation valid from 22 September 2026. Add EUR 350,000 of capital and the running costs of a regulated firm. DNB has flagged a change to its fees from 11 January 2027.

How long does it take to get an EMI licence in the Netherlands?

DNB has three months from a complete application. It says total processing tends to be longer, because the clock starts only when the file is complete and stops while DNB waits for information. We do not quote a real-world average.

Do I need people in the Netherlands?

Yes. DNB expects at least two day-to-day policymakers who work from the Netherlands, and a substantial presence of the management board and key function staff there.

What is the Dutch exemption for small e-money issuers?

A registration without a licence for issuers with outstanding e-money up to EUR 5,000,000, at most EUR 150 per instrument or account, no agents and clean manager antecedents. The issuer is not supervised by DNB and cannot passport.

Can I file in English?

DNB publishes its application notes and process pages in English. We did not find a written language rule, so confirm with DNB at the pre-application contact.

Can I passport a Dutch EMI licence?

Yes, a licensed EGI notifies DNB, which informs the host state (PSD2 Art 28). An exempt issuer cannot.

What is the difference between a betaalinstelling and an EGI?

A payment institution [betaalinstelling] provides payment services only and cannot issue e-money. An EGI issues e-money and may also provide payment services, with EUR 350,000 of capital. See the payment institution page.
